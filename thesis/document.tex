\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Accurate prediction of water levels in Mediterranean ephemeral streams is essential for flood early warning and wetland ecosystem management. This thesis focuses on the Sant Miquel basin (Mallorca, Spain), whose catchment drains into the ecologically sensitive Sa~Marjal coastal wetland (monitoring station STM08). Two complementary modelling paradigms are developed and evaluated: (\textit{i})~a physics-based hydrological model constructed with HEC-HMS (Hydrologic Engineering Center -- Hydrologic Modeling System) and calibrated against twelve years of sub-hourly streamflow observations from eight monitoring stations; and (\textit{ii})~a suite of data-driven models -- including gradient-boosted trees, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, and the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) -- trained on the same multi-source observational record. Model performance is assessed with standard hydrological metrics (Nash--Sutcliffe Efficiency, Kling--Gupta Efficiency, RMSE, PBIAS) across flood events, recession periods, and ordinary flow conditions. The comparative analysis aims to identify the conditions under which each paradigm offers the greatest advantage and to provide operational guidance for real-time forecasting. The ultimate objective is the integration of the resulting forecaster into the RiscBal application, where the predicted water level will be displayed as an additional curve alongside the observed record at Sa~Marjal.
\end{abstract}



\section{Introduction\label{sec:intro}}

Flash floods rank among the most destructive natural hazards in Mediterranean Europe, responsible for significant loss of life and economic damage each year~\cite{Llasat2010}. Their defining characteristics -- short response times, high peak discharges, and rapid recession -- are determined by the interaction of intense convective or frontal rainfall with small, steep, low-permeability catchments. The Balearic Islands experience some of the highest unit-area flood peaks recorded in Europe~\cite{Estrany2010}, driven by Mesoscale Convective Systems whose intensity is amplified by warm sea-surface temperatures and island orography.

Against this backdrop, real-time prediction of streamflow is both an ecological and a public-safety imperative. Ecologically, the Sa~Marjal coastal wetland -- the terminal receptor of the Sant Miquel basin -- is a protected habitat under the EU Habitats Directive (92/43/EEC) whose ecological functioning depends on the timing and magnitude of flood pulses. From a risk management perspective, the lower reaches of the basin are subject to recurring flood damage, making timely warnings critical for civil protection.

Two broad paradigms exist for hydrological streamflow modelling. \textit{Physics-based models} represent catchment processes -- infiltration, surface runoff generation, channel routing -- through governing equations grounded in physical principles~\cite{Beven2012}. Tools such as HEC-HMS, SWAT, and TOPMODEL are widely used in operational hydrology. Their main advantages are physical interpretability, the ability to incorporate catchment characteristics derived from terrain analysis and field surveys, and a capacity for extrapolation to unobserved scenarios. Their limitations include structural parameter uncertainty, high data requirements for catchment characterisation, and sensitivity to spatial variability in precipitation.

\textit{Data-driven models}, by contrast, learn the input--output mapping directly from historical observations without explicit process representation. Classical approaches such as random forests and gradient-boosted trees are computationally efficient and robust to missing data~\cite{Breiman2001}. Deep learning architectures -- particularly LSTM networks~\cite{Hochreiter1997} and the Temporal Fusion Transformer~\cite{Lim2021} -- excel at capturing long-range temporal dependencies and multivariate covariates. Recent large-scale benchmarks have demonstrated that deep learning models can match or exceed physics-based models when trained on sufficient data~\cite{Kratzert2019}. Their main limitations are poor extrapolation beyond the training distribution and limited physical interpretability.

The complementarity of these paradigms motivates systematic comparative evaluations on well-instrumented catchments. This thesis contributes to that research agenda by applying both approaches to the Sant Miquel basin, using a continuous monitoring record maintained since 2012 by the RiscBal hydrological risk observatory of the Universitat de les Illes Balears. The dataset provides sub-hourly precipitation, temperature, water level, discharge, and suspended sediment concentration at eight stations, complemented by three hourly rain-gauge records from the Spanish national meteorological agency (AEMET). The twelve-year observation window encompasses a wide spectrum of hydrological conditions, including multiple extreme events, constituting a rigorous test bed for the comparison. The work has, in addition, an explicitly applied objective: the best-performing model is intended to be integrated into the RiscBal application, so that the water-level chart of station STM08 displays an additional curve with the predicted level, converting a monitoring platform into an early-warning tool.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:area} characterises the study area. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the monitoring network, data architecture, and quality issues. Section~\ref{sec:model} defines the physics-based model. Section~\ref{sec:problem} formalises the prediction problem and its operational context.



\section{Study Area\label{sec:area}}

The Sant Miquel basin is located in northern Mallorca, Spain (approximately 39.80\textdegree{}N, 2.95\textdegree{}E), within the Serra de Tramuntana mountain range -- a UNESCO World Heritage landscape. The catchment is underlain primarily by Mesozoic karstic limestone, resulting in a partly subsurface drainage network that complicates runoff estimation from terrain analysis alone. Surface topography is rugged, with elevations ranging from near sea level at the Sa~Marjal wetland to above 600~m in the headwaters, and channel gradients are steep.

The climate is semi-arid Mediterranean (K\"oppen BSk/Csa). Mean annual precipitation varies from approximately 800~mm in the Serra de Tramuntana highlands to 400--500~mm near the coast. Rainfall is strongly seasonal: the bulk of the annual total falls between October and March in association with Atlantic frontal systems and Mediterranean convective events; summers are hot and dry. Interannual variability is high. The hydrological regime is intermittent -- the main channel (Torrent de Sant Miquel) and its tributaries carry flow only during and shortly after rainfall events, since baseflow is negligible owing to rapid drainage through karst conduits. Land use is dominated by natural garrigue scrubland and traditional rain-fed agriculture (almond and olive orchards), with low impervious cover.

The terminal node of the catchment is the Sa~Marjal wetland (STM08), a Site of Community Importance (SCI) under the Natura~2000 network. Near-real-time hydrological data from the monitoring network are publicly accessible through the RiscBal application (\url{https://riscbal.uib.es}), which serves as the operational context for the forecasting system developed in this thesis.

\subsection{Data architecture}

Each observation in the monitoring database is described by the following attributes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Timestamp (UTC).} Date and time of measurement, stored in Coordinated Universal Time to prevent ambiguities arising from daylight saving time transitions.
    \item \textbf{Sensor identifier.} A unique code linking each record to a specific instrument at a specific station, enabling instrument-level quality control and detection of sensor drift between equipment generations.
    \item \textbf{Measured value.} The physical quantity recorded (water level, precipitation depth, temperature, etc.) in standard hydrological units (m, mm, \textdegree{}C, m$^{3}$~s$^{-1}$).
    \item \textbf{Geographic coordinates.} Latitude, longitude, and altitude of each sensor, enabling spatial operations such as Thiessen polygon construction for areal rainfall estimation and integration with digital elevation models for HEC-HMS parameterisation.
    \item \textbf{Sensor model.} Manufacturer and instrument designation, relevant for assessing systematic biases between successive instrument generations and for consistency checks after equipment replacement.
    \item \textbf{Water temperature.} Recorded alongside hydraulic variables at hydrological stations and captured in the dataset (clean CSVs, 10-minute grid, and measurements table); it is not used as a model predictor owing to multi-year sensor outages (notably absent at STM08 from 2016 to 2023).
\end{itemize}

This normalised schema supports both the spatial operations required for physics-based model parameterisation and the tabular feature engineering required for machine learning.

\subsection{Data quality and challenges}

The common observation window after temporal alignment spans approximately twelve years (September~2014 to July~2026). A systematic exploratory data analysis (EDA) was conducted over the full historical record and revealed four interrelated quality issues that shape the preprocessing and modelling strategy.

\subsubsection{Mixed temporal resolutions}

STM stations originally sampled at 15-minute intervals; the frequency was upgraded to 10~min in May~2022 and to 5~min at most stations from mid-2025 onward, while AEMET gauges provide hourly data. Degrading all series to the coarsest (hourly) resolution would discard precisely the fast-response dynamics that characterise flash floods in small Mediterranean catchments. The modelling dataset is therefore built on a \textbf{uniform 10-minute grid} (2014-09-26 to 2026-07-02, bounded by the AEMET record), selected as the finest resolution at which all variables can be meaningfully represented given the available data density. The resampling strategy distinguishes between two physical measurement types:

\textit{Instantaneous quantities (HEIGHT\_m, TEMP\_C, WATER\_TEMP\_C).} Water level and temperature (air and water) are point measurements whose value at time $t$ represents the state at that instant, independent of the preceding sampling interval. For these variables, the following transformations are applied:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{5~min $\rightarrow$ 10~min:} arithmetic mean of two consecutive values. This avoids artificially introducing variance from the higher-frequency record while preserving the average dynamics over the 10-minute window.
    \item \textbf{15~min $\rightarrow$ 10~min:} linear interpolation with a gap cap of 3~h. Interpolation is justified because the 15-minute rate already captures the sub-hourly variability of Mediterranean flash floods, and the introduction of intermediate points at 10-minute spacing does not fabricate new extrema beyond those observable in the coarser record.
    \item \textbf{10~min $\rightarrow$ 10~min:} direct alignment (no transformation).
\end{itemize}

\textit{Cumulative quantities (PRECIP\_mm).} Precipitation is reported as the accumulated depth over the preceding sampling interval. Treating it as an instantaneous value (e.g.\ by simple linear interpolation of rain amounts) would violate mass conservation: a 15-minute total of 3~mm would be spread across two 10-minute slots, yielding spurious rainfall during rainless periods. The adopted approach converts each cumulative series to an accumulated-depth curve ($P(t) = \sum_{\tau \leq t} p(\tau)$), interpolates this curve onto the 10-minute grid, and differentiates back to recover 10-minute accumulation depths. This method is mass-conserving by construction: the integral of the disaggregated series over any source interval exactly equals the original total.

\textit{Hourly-to-10-minute AEMET disaggregation.} Naively dividing hourly precipitation totals by six assumes uniform rainfall intensity throughout the hour, thereby attenuating peak intensities -- a critical distortion when the same hour contains both intense convective bursts and quiescent periods. A \textbf{template-based disaggregation} scheme is implemented instead. For each hourly AEMET record, the algorithm searches for a contemporaneous 10-minute precipitation pattern from either STM01 or STM02 (both of which operate at the target resolution) and distributes the hourly total proportionally to that temporal template. When neither STM station reports precipitation in the same hour, the algorithm falls back to the conservative cumulative-curve interpolation described above. This hybrid strategy exploits the physical coherence of the sub-daily rainfall field (convective cells have spatial extent) while guaranteeing mass conservation in all cases.

\subsubsection{Missing records and quality flags}

Sensor malfunctions, maintenance periods, and telecommunication failures generate data gaps of varying duration. Two principal missing-data regimes exist:

\textbf{Short gaps ($\leq$ 24~h):} linear interpolation fills gaps of up to 24~h on the 10-minute grid. Water level and temperature are interpolated linearly in time; precipitation increments are interpolated linearly as well, so that every observed value remains untouched. Filled cells are tagged $\texttt{DATA\_TYPE} = \texttt{imputed}$; in total 21\,156 cells are recovered this way.

\textbf{Long gaps ($>$ 24~h):} no interpolation is attempted. The corresponding 10-minute grid cells are left as \texttt{NaN} and paired with a companion $\texttt{\{col\}\_MISSING}$ (0/1) indicator column, so downstream models can distinguish ``missing'' from ``zero''. Discharge is excluded from imputation entirely, since a \texttt{NaN} there indicates that the water level fell outside the rating-curve range rather than that data are absent. The frequency of extended gaps varies by station: STM02\_TEMP\_C shows the highest null rate at 36.2~\%, while STM08\_HEIGHT\_m (the target station) exhibits the lowest at 0.7~\%.

Data quality is encoded through a \texttt{QUALITY} flag preserved in all clean CSV files and carried into the 10-minute grid. The flag meanings, provided by the data manager, are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{0:} data validated as correct (good).
    \item \textbf{1:} data marked as suspicious and requiring expert review.
    \item \textbf{2:} data identified as erroneous (wrong).
    \item \textbf{empty:} quality flag not available (historical data sourced from Excel files rather than the production database).
\end{itemize}

A companion \texttt{DATA\_TYPE} column tags each observation with one of five provenance labels: \texttt{observed} (measured, including re-gridding to the common 10-minute grid), \texttt{corrected} (measured but fixed for a known error, e.g.\ the STM02 tipping-bucket formula recovery or the diurnal drift correction of Section~\ref{sec:harmonization}), \texttt{imputed} (missing value filled by interpolation), \texttt{derived} (computed deterministically from other measurements, e.g.\ discharge via rating curves and AEMET precipitation disaggregated from hourly totals), and \texttt{simulated} (model output, reserved for the HEC-HMS and machine-learning forecasts). The labels are assigned per variable in the 10-minute grid and propagate through the pipeline into the measurements table.

Two additional quality issues were identified and resolved in the production-DB extension period (2025--2026). First, the database exports water level in \textbf{centimetres}, which was initially ingested as metres; after conversion ($\div 100$), verified against overlapping Excel records at identical timestamps, the extension becomes physically consistent. Second, the database flags 51\,336 STM03 records and 6\,888 STM05 records as suspicious ($\texttt{QUALITY} = 1$) in this period; a continuity audit showed both blocks to be consistent with neighbouring validated data (level jumps at segment boundaries of 3~mm and 35~mm respectively), so they are retained, whereas the 10\,026 erroneous ($\texttt{QUALITY} = 2$) STM05 records are discarded at ingestion.

\subsubsection{Incomplete variable coverage in the extended period}

In the DB extension period (from mid-2025 onward) only \texttt{HEIGHT\_m} is available from the hydrological stations' raw telemetry. Since discharge is derived from water level via the station rating curves during preprocessing (Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}), \texttt{DISCHARGE} remains available throughout the record; the raw \texttt{VOLUME} and \texttt{LOAD} channels of the logger export are not continued. Because \texttt{HEIGHT\_m} is the target variable and drives all derived quantities, this does not affect the prediction task.

\subsubsection{Highly intermittent flow regime}

Discharge is zero for the majority of the record (85\,\% of validated measurements at STM08). The resulting heavily right-skewed distribution of \texttt{HEIGHT\_m} means that standard loss functions minimising average error will implicitly favour dry-period performance over flood events. Metric selection, class-stratified evaluation, and event-based analysis are therefore essential components of the model assessment framework.

The resulting 10-minute grid contains 618\,769 timestamps over approximately 11.8~years: 19 measured variables (six \texttt{HEIGHT\_m}, six \texttt{WATER\_TEMP\_C}, two air \texttt{TEMP\_C}, five \texttt{PRECIP\_mm}), six derived \texttt{DISCHARGE} series, eleven quality-flag columns, and per-variable provenance labels. Discharge is derived from water level via station-specific piecewise-polynomial rating curves $\texttt{Q(H)} = \sum_i \text{base}_i \cdot H^{\,\text{exp}_i}$ parameterised over 1--3 segments per station using drone-based cross-section surveys; the curves are applied over the full record, including the DB extension period. The grid is stored as a compressed Parquet file and constitutes the single entry point for imputation, event detection, and feature engineering.

\subsection{Feature engineering\label{sec:features}}

The 10-minute grid is transformed into a supervised learning table through four categories of engineered predictors, following established practices in hydrological machine learning~\cite{Kratzert2019}.

\subsubsection{Lagged predictors}

For each measurement variable at each station, the raw value is shifted backward in time to create a look-back window at lags of 1, 2, 3, 6, 12, and 24 hours. This yields 78 lagged columns: 36 for water level (six hydro stations), 12 for air temperature (two meteo stations), and 30 for precipitation (five stations). These lags allow the model to capture the propagation delay of flow through the channel network and the memory effect of antecedent moisture conditions. The choice of 24 hours as the maximum lag is supported by the EDA cross-correlation analysis, which found the strongest linear relationships at lags of 30~min to 1~h and negligible correlation beyond approximately 12~h.

Discharge lags are intentionally excluded: since $\texttt{DISCHARGE} = f(\texttt{HEIGHT})$ via the monotonic rating curve, including both would introduce exact functional multicollinearity without adding information~\cite{Kratzert2019}. Instead, a single basin-integrated discharge metric is constructed (see below).

\subsubsection{Cumulative precipitation}

The antecedent precipitation index (API) encodes catchment wetness as the rolling sum of precipitation over windows of 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 hours, computed for each of the five precipitation stations (25 columns). The API has been shown to be a critical predictor for runoff generation in semi-arid Mediterranean basins, where initial soil moisture strongly modulates the rainfall--runoff response~\cite{Estrany2010}.

\subsubsection{Discharge-based flow features}

The confirmed drainage topology of the Sant Miquel network (from the geo-morphological analysis) is used to construct physically meaningful flow predictors. The main channel runs STM03 $\rightarrow$ STM04 $\rightarrow$ STM06 $\rightarrow$ STM08 in series, with STM05 a tributary merging into STM06 and STM07 a tributary reaching STM08 directly. Three families of flow features are derived:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Individual discharge.} The discharge series of the five upstream stations (STM03--STM07) are lagged at 1, 2, 3, 6, 12, and 24~h, mirroring the water-level lags (30 columns). The individual flows are retained rather than collapsed into a single aggregate because the karstic system does not always behave as a clean series routing: conduit losses and subsurface transfer mean the raw flows carry information that simple differences alone do not.
    \item \textbf{Lateral inflows.} Inter-station flow differences quantify the runoff gained along each reach: $\Delta Q_{03,04} = Q_{\mathrm{STM04}} - Q_{\mathrm{STM03}}$ along the STM03--STM04 reach, and $\Delta Q_{05,06} = Q_{\mathrm{STM06}} - Q_{\mathrm{STM04}} - Q_{\mathrm{STM05}}$ into STM06 after accounting for its two upstream contributors. Each is lagged identically to the raw series (14 columns).
    \item \textbf{Total wetland inflow.} The basin-integrated inflow to Sa~Marjal is $\mathrm{Q\_IN\_STM08} = Q_{\mathrm{STM06}} + Q_{\mathrm{STM07}}$, lagged at the same six horizons (7 columns). This corrects an earlier formulation, $\mathrm{UPSTREAM\_Q} = \sum_{i=3}^{7} Q_{\mathrm{STM}i}$, which implicitly assumed five parallel tributaries and therefore double-counted the series stations STM03, STM04, and STM06.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Temporal encoding}

Cyclical time features use sine--cosine encoding: $\sin(2\pi h/24)$ and $\cos(2\pi h/24)$ for hour of day, and $\sin(2\pi d/365.25)$ and $\cos(2\pi d/365.25)$ for day of year. This representation preserves the circular topology of daily and annual cycles, ensuring that 23:00 and 01:00 are adjacent in feature space -- a property that raw integer encoding violates~\cite{Lim2021}. The month is included as a raw integer (1--12), appropriate as a categorical variable for tree-based models and as an embedding input for deep learning architectures.

\subsubsection{Target definition}

The prediction target is the water level at STM08, shifted forward in time to produce three multi-horizon target columns: $h(t{+}\mathrm{1h})$, $h(t{+}\mathrm{6h})$, and $h(t{+}\mathrm{24h})$. This corresponds to leads of 6, 36, and 144 time steps on the 10-minute grid. Rows where any target is undefined (the trailing 24 hours of the series) are dropped, yielding a final training table of 614\,201 rows and 289 columns (255 predictors after excluding targets, split labels, and metadata columns).

\subsection{Event detection\label{sec:events}}

Model evaluation is performed both on the full chronological test period and exclusively on identified flood events, to assess whether forecast skill degrades during the rare high-flow conditions that carry the greatest operational consequence.

Flood events are identified using a \textbf{Peak Over Threshold} (POT) approach with an independence criterion, a standard methodology in flood frequency analysis~\cite{Claps2003, Burn2016}. The detection operates on the 10-minute discharge series at STM08 ($\texttt{DISCHARGE\_m$^{3}$\,s$^{-1}$}$) and proceeds through five stages:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Core identification.} Timestamps where discharge exceeds $Q_{\mathrm{thresh}} = 0.2$~m$^{3}$\,s$^{-1}$ are flagged as potential event cores. Consecutive exceedances are grouped, allowing gaps of up to 3~h within a single core to accommodate the multi-peak structure of Mediterranean convective storms.
    \item \textbf{Independence filtering.} Core groups closer than 6~h are merged into a single event. Groups separated by more than 6~h are treated as independent events.
    \item \textbf{Boundary expansion.} Each event is expanded backward (up to 12~h) to capture the rising limb, and forward (up to 24~h) to capture the recession, using a lower threshold of 0.05~m$^{3}$\,s$^{-1}$ with a 1~h persistence requirement for event termination.
    \item \textbf{Duration capping.} Cores exceeding 72~h (indicative of sustained wet-season baseflow rather than discrete events) are capped to prevent multi-month event characterisation.
    \item \textbf{Minimum peak level.} As a global validity criterion applied uniformly to the whole record, events whose peak water level at STM08 is below 0.10~m are discarded and the remaining events renumbered. This excludes sub-flood pulses such as residual sensor thermal-drift oscillations in the DB-extension period (Section~\ref{sec:harmonization}) as well as genuinely minor level fluctuations, ensuring that the event catalogue contains only hydrologically meaningful floods regardless of data source.
\end{enumerate}

The detection yields 175 valid events over the 11.8-year analysis window, with a mean duration of 34.9~h (median 20.0~h, maximum 108~h). The largest historical peak reaches 87.8~m$^{3}$\,s$^{-1}$ (corresponding to a water level of 2.83~m at STM08), recorded during the January~2017 storm. Events are distributed across the chronological split as follows: 102 training events (Sep~2014--Dec~2020), 16 validation events (Jan~2021--Jun~2022), and 57 test events (Jul~2022--Jul~2026), including 17 events observed by the DB extension after July~2025.

Each event is characterised by its start, peak, and end timestamps; peak discharge and water level; duration; total accumulated precipitation across all stations; maximum hourly rainfall intensity; and its split assignment. An $\texttt{event\_id}$ column is appended to the training table (nullable integer, NaN for non-event rows), enabling per-event model evaluation without temporal leakage across event boundaries.

\subsection{Extension data: unit conversion and harmonization\label{sec:harmonization}}

The DB extension period (mid-2025 to mid-2026) extends the analysis window by approximately one year but required two corrections before it could be merged with the validated record.

\subsubsection{Unit conversion}

Cross-validation of the database export against the Excel record at identical timestamps revealed that the production database stores water level in \textbf{centimetres}: e.g.\ at the 2025-08-28 flood peak, the Excel-validated level is 0.372~m while the DB value reads 37.5. Ingesting the raw values as metres produced apparent levels of 3--5~m during dry periods and spurious discharge estimates up to 176~m$^{3}$\,s$^{-1}$ -- an artefact initially misdiagnosed as a sensor datum shift. After converting to metres ($\div 100$), extension levels agree with overlapping validated records to within a few millimetres.

\subsubsection{Quality filtering}

Of the extension records, 10\,026 STM05 values flagged $\texttt{QUALITY} = 2$ (erroneous) are discarded at ingestion. Records flagged $\texttt{QUALITY} = 1$ (suspicious) are retained after a continuity audit: 51\,336 STM03 records (half of its extension) form blocks continuous with neighbouring validated data (maximum level jump at segment boundaries 3~mm), and 6\,888 STM05 records behave likewise (35~mm).

\subsubsection{Diurnal drift correction}

Inspection of the hour-of-day level climatology revealed that the DB-only segments of four stations exhibit a spurious daily oscillation absent from the historical record -- a signature of pressure-transducer thermal drift rather than hydrology, since the level \emph{peaks} at midday whereas evaporative losses would lower it. The correction is applied uniformly to every station: the hour-of-day climatologies of quiet rows (level $< 0.1$~m) in the DB-only segment and in a season-matched historical reference are compared, their centred difference is subtracted from the DB-only rows if -- and only if -- the drift amplitude exceeds 5~mm and the DB-only diurnal range exceeds the historical range by a factor of five; otherwise the station is left untouched. Four stations trigger the correction (STM04: $5.0$ vs $0.5$~mm; STM05: $10.6$ vs $1.1$~mm; STM07: $26.1$ vs $0.8$~mm; STM08: $32.9$ vs $1.2$~mm), reducing the target station's diurnal range from 27.8~mm to 5.2~mm, while STM03 (whose 44~mm diurnal cycle is genuine evaporative signal) and STM06 pass unchanged. Corrected cells are tagged $\texttt{DATA\_TYPE} = \texttt{corrected}$ for traceability.

\section{The Physics-Based Model\label{sec:model}}

The physics-based paradigm is represented by HEC-HMS (Hydrologic Engineering Center -- Hydrologic Modeling System), a widely used semi-distributed rainfall--runoff modelling software developed by the US Army Corps of Engineers~\cite{USACE2000}. HEC-HMS represents a catchment as a network of hydrologic elements -- sub-basins, reaches, junctions, and reservoirs -- connected according to the drainage topology. Within each sub-basin, the transformation of precipitation into runoff is decomposed into sequential, separately parameterised processes: (\textit{i})~a \textit{loss} component, which partitions precipitation into infiltration and effective rainfall; (\textit{ii})~a \textit{transform} component, which converts effective rainfall into direct runoff at the sub-basin outlet; and (\textit{iii})~a \textit{routing} component, which propagates the resulting hydrograph through the channel network, accounting for flow translation and attenuation.

For the Sant Miquel basin, the model will be delineated and parameterised using the available high-resolution digital elevation model and the land-use and soil-type cartography, from which sub-basin morphometry and loss parameters are derived. Precipitation forcing will combine the STM01--STM02 gauges with the temporally disaggregated AEMET records. Calibration and validation will be performed against observed water levels and discharges at the interior gauges (STM03--STM07), which act as internal control points, and at the basin outlet (STM08), the latter providing the direct comparison with the data-driven family over the same events and evaluation periods.



\section{Problem Statement\label{sec:problem}}

Let $h(t)$ denote the water level (m) recorded at STM08 at discrete time step $t$ on the uniform 10-minute grid. Let $\mathbf{x}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{p}$, $p = 255$, be the vector of $p$ predictor variables at time $t$, comprising 10-minute precipitation depths, upstream water levels and discharges, air temperatures, cumulative antecedent precipitation indices, discharge-based flow features (lateral inflows and total wetland inflow), missingness indicators, and temporal features (hour-of-day, day-of-year, and month encoding). The prediction task is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \hat{h}(t + \Delta) = f\!\left(\mathbf{x}(t),\, \mathbf{x}(t{-}1),\, \ldots,\, \mathbf{x}(t{-}L)\right)
    \label{eq:problem}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\Delta$ is the forecast horizon, an integer multiple of the 10-minute base interval, $L$ is the look-back window in time steps, and $f(\cdot)$ is the model function. Two instantiations of $f$ are investigated: (i)~the physics-based HEC-HMS model, in which $f$ encapsulates calibrated process equations for rainfall--runoff transformation and channel routing; and (ii)~a family of data-driven models, in which $f$ is learned from observations by statistical optimisation.

The supervised dataset comprises 614\,201 training samples over the period September~2014 to July~2026, each with 255 predictor features and three multi-horizon targets $h(t{+}\mathrm{1h})$, $h(t{+}\mathrm{6h})$, and $h(t{+}\mathrm{24h})$. The dataset is partitioned chronologically to avoid temporal leakage:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training:} September~2014 -- December~2020
    \item \textbf{Validation:} January~2021 -- June~2022
    \item \textbf{Test:} July~2022 -- July~2026
\end{itemize}

A total of 175 flood events are identified across the analysis window (102 training, 16 validation, 57 test), supporting per-event evaluation in addition to aggregate time-series metrics.

Model performance is quantified using four metrics standard in operational hydrology: Nash--Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE), Kling--Gupta Efficiency (KGE), root mean squared error (RMSE), and percent bias (PBIAS). Results are reported separately for the full test period and for identified flood events, to distinguish performance on rare high-flow conditions from overall average accuracy.

Beyond the comparative evaluation, the problem is framed by its operational endpoint: the selected forecaster will be embedded in the RiscBal application, where at each time step the predicted level $\hat{h}(t+\Delta)$ will be plotted as an additional curve on the STM08 water-level chart alongside the observations, converting the monitoring platform into an early-warning tool for flood risk management in the lower basin.
